Section 2 recap: A cloud provider rents computing on demand so a company pays rather than buys, and the company that builds a foundation model is not the one that hosts it; software calls a model through a published interface (API) and gets an answer back, while best-practice guidance such as the AWS Cloud Adoption Framework is followed, not switched on. An AI solution is more than a model — it includes the data, systems, and people around it: an algorithm finds patterns, a model is the trained result you deploy, training builds it once, inference runs on every request, and every prediction is a likely answer, so design the process around wrong ones. Generative AI sits inside machine learning, which sits inside the wider AI field; a foundation model is trained on broad data and serves many use cases, turning model-building into buying access — Amazon Bedrock for managed access to models from several providers, Amazon SageMaker AI for building and training your own — and that choice decides how much of the solution you own. On data: structured data has a fixed shape, unstructured (documents, images, audio) does not, most of what a company holds is unstructured, the shape of the data points to the kind of AI that fits, a model is only as good as the data it learned from, and coverage gaps make it least accurate for the least-represented group. On feasibility and lifecycle: AI cannot serve a use case where the outcome was never recorded, a model trained on past data assumes the future resembles the past, accuracy falls as live data drifts so monitoring never stops, bias can sit in prediction behaviour as well as training data, and baselines recorded before launch are what drift and bias get measured against. On standards: ISO/IEC 23053 frames how to describe an ML-based AI system, ISO/IEC 42001 is the certifiable AI management system standard, ISO/IEC 22989 supplies the shared terminology — so ask a vendor which one they follow, because each promises something different.
